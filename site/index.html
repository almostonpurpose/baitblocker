02 Why this exists

The short version

Persuasion in an interface is a documented craft. Thaler and Sunstein called the arrangement around a decision its choice architecture — which option is preselected, what sits in front of you, what is buried. Nothing is taken from you and the outcome still moves.

Cialdini catalogued the levers aimed at you directly: scarcity, social proof, authority, commitment, reciprocity, liking. Tversky and Kahneman showed that two identical descriptions of a fact produce different decisions. Loewenstein's information gap explains the withheld headline — open a gap and closing it starts to feel urgent.

Harry Brignull named the applied version dark patterns in 2010. Mathur and colleagues found them on roughly one in nine of 11,000 shopping sites, and the FTC and the EU's Digital Services Act now write rules against parts of the practice.

BaitBlocker matches phrasings these mechanisms tend to produce: a rough instrument pointed at a real thing. It is rule-based, so it cannot establish intent, judge ideology, or catch what only emerges across a multi-step checkout.

Where each category comes from
  • Pressure Thaler & Sunstein, Nudge (2008) · Cialdini on scarcity · Brignull (2010) · Mathur et al. (2019)
  • Influence Cialdini, Influence (1984)
  • Engagement bait Loewenstein (1994) · Simon on attention (1971) · Fogg (2003)
  • Framing Tversky & Kahneman, Science (1981)

03 What it sends

Nothing

BaitBlocker makes no network requests. The rules, the fonts and the explanations all ship inside the extension. Page text is read in the page and never copied off your machine.

It stores two things locally: your own settings, and a count of how many pages have been scanned and how many patterns were marked. No URLs, no page text, no account. The full policy runs to about a page.
